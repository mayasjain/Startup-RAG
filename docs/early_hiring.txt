Hiring Your First 10 Employees

The first 10 hires define your company's culture and trajectory more than almost any other decision. Get this right.

Who to hire first:
- Only hire when you have a specific, painful gap you cannot fill yourself
- The first hires should be generalists who can do multiple things
- Avoid hiring managers before you have a team for them to manage
- Engineers and people who directly build or sell the product first

The bar:
- Every hire should raise the average. If you're unsure, don't hire.
- Paul Graham's test: would this person be a founder? If yes, hire them.
- Hire for hunger and learning speed over credentials at early stage
- Culture fit matters: would you want to be stuck in an airport with this person?

Where to find early hires:
- Your personal network is the best source
- Former colleagues who you've worked with and trust
- Referrals from your existing team
- YC's Work at a Startup, Wellfound (AngelList Talent)
- Twitter/X and LinkedIn for niche technical roles

Compensation at early stage:
- Salaries will be below market — offset with equity
- Typical early engineer: 0.5%–2% equity depending on stage and role
- First non-founder hire at pre-seed: can be up to 1-2%
- Use a 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff as standard

Red flags to avoid:
- Candidates who optimize for title over impact
- People who need a lot of process and structure to function
- Anyone who talks more about what they won't do than what they will
- Candidates who have never worked in a fast-moving environment

Firing fast:
- Most founders wait too long to fire underperformers
- A bad hire in a 5-person team is catastrophic — act quickly
- Give clear feedback first, but if no change in 2-3 weeks, move on
- Keeping a bad hire "to avoid conflict" is unfair to the rest of the team

Remote vs in-person:
- In-person is strongly preferred for the first 10 hires if possible
- Co-location accelerates communication speed dramatically at early stage
- If remote, default hours and over-communicate on async tools
